What If Someday Started With an Hour?

Making room for the creative work that keeps calling you back.

There is a whisper on your heart.

Maybe it has been there for years. Maybe it arrived recently, but something about it feels familiar. A book you want to write. An art you want to learn. A problem you keep imagining a solution to. An idea that returns while you’re walking, driving, or drifting off to sleep.

You have every intention of getting to it.

After work settles down. After the kids need less. After you reach the next milestone. When you retire. When there is finally room.

Many of us were raised with some version of the same instruction: clean your plate before you have dessert. Finish what is required before reaching for what you want.

It is an understandable approach to dinner. As a way of organizing an entire life, it leaves something important waiting.

Because the plate keeps filling up.

There is always another responsibility, another deadline, another person who needs something. If creative expression depends on having nothing else left to do, we may never begin.

The part of you that wants to make something

As work becomes increasingly shaped by data, efficiency, and productivity, it is worth asking where we find room for meaning.

AI can help us produce, organize, and move through tasks. It can also make it easy to hand over more of our thinking without pausing to notice which parts we enjoy doing ourselves. Amid all that output, we still need opportunities to follow our curiosity, make choices, experiment, and discover what we have to say.

There is a particular satisfaction in bringing something into the world that began with your own attention.

Your life is already a tapestry of things you have made: relationships, meals, homes, solutions, traditions, moments of care. Creativity runs through all of it. And there may still be one particular thread you have wanted to follow.

That project deserves a place in your life while you are living it.

You do not have to know whether it will become a business, a published book, or anything anyone else will ever see. Being curious about it is enough reason to begin.

A microdose of creativity

You probably aren’t going to stop everything and reorganize your life around this longing tomorrow.

You can, however, give it an hour.

One hour this week, set aside for the thing you keep saying you want to do.

Small enough to fit into the life you have. Long enough to make contact with an idea that has been living at a distance.

If you want to write a novel, spend that hour getting to know a character. What do they want? What are they afraid to admit? Write one scene without worrying about where it belongs.

If you want to paint, put the materials on the table and make a few marks. Let yourself learn what the paint does.

If you have an idea for solving a problem, sketch it. Write down who experiences it and what might make their life easier.

Imagining counts. Research can count, too. Give yourself a little time to explore, then try making something, however rough: a paragraph, a sketch, a recording, a first attempt.

Leave yourself something to return to.

Make the next beginning easier

Before your hour ends, write one sentence:

Next time, I want to…

Finish the scene. Try another color. Talk to someone who has this problem. Learn the next three chords.

Then put another hour on the calendar.

The value of this small practice grows through returning. You begin to understand the work. You discover what interests you enough to keep going. An idea that once felt enormous becomes a series of things you can try.

Some weeks will be awkward. You may spend an hour on something you later change completely. You may find that the project wants to become something different from what you imagined.

That is useful knowledge. You gain it by spending time with the work.

As you continue, notice how that hour feels. Does it leave you more curious? More present? Does some part of you feel relieved to have finally begun?

From there, you can decide whether to make more room. Perhaps another hour each week. Perhaps an occasional afternoon.

Let the practice grow in a way your life can hold.

When you want to stay a little longer

An hour can open a door. Eventually, you may want enough uninterrupted time to walk through it and see what is there.

Time to hold a whole story in your mind. To experiment beyond the first attempt. To follow an idea without having to pack it away just as you begin to find your way.

I know those nudges well. They were what led me to use my annual beach trip to explore a different topic, idea, or possibility that had been calling to me.

Those weeks became some of my favorites because I shared them with another person who was also seeking, exploring, and trying things out.

We would spend long mornings over coffee, talking about what we wanted to explore. We asked questions, imagined possibilities, and helped each other think through ideas that were still taking shape. Then we would head to our own spaces to learn, make, and experiment.

Later, we would come back together and share what had happened: what we had tried, what surprised us, where we were getting stuck. Those conversations often gave us something new to try the next day.

I discovered how much can happen in a week when you give an idea your attention. I also found myself valuing the space itself as much as anything I accomplished. I had time to be curious, to follow a question, to try something without needing to know where it would lead.

And the feeling stayed with me after I came home.

I wanted more of those weeks. More mornings spent talking about ideas over coffee. More time to work on something that mattered to me, with people nearby who were doing the same.

That is what led me to create Your Radical Sabbatical: a week devoted to a personal creative pursuit, in the company of other people making room for their own.

I want to share the experience that made those beach weeks so meaningful to me. I crave being around people who are taking their ideas off the shelf, dusting them off, and wholeheartedly giving them time and energy.

The first gathering is planned for June 2027 in Beverly, Massachusetts. In a seaside setting, each person will bring an idea, project, or goal to explore, with room for both individual creative time and the conversations that help us find our way.

Some may leave with something finished. Others may leave with a clearer direction, a promising beginning, or a better understanding of what comes next. Each will have spent a week giving attention to something that matters to them.

If you have an idea on your heart that could use that kind of space, I’d love to hear from you. Express your interest in Your Radical Sabbatical to learn more as the gathering takes shape.

And wherever that leads, you can begin this week.

Choose an hour. Put it on your calendar. Bring the idea as it is.

You have carried it this far. Give yourself a little time to see what it might become.
